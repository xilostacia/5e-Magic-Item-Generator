Waybreaker is a heavy dwarven warhammer with a square head of dark steel and a haft wrapped in old brown leather. The metal head is split by several deep cracks that should have rendered the weapon useless centuries ago. None of them have widened, no fragments have ever broken free, and even direct attempts to repair the damage fail. Fresh metal simply flakes away until the original cracks are visible again.

The hammer was created by dwarven miners after a collapse trapped an entire work crew behind several dozen feet of stone. The rescue took days, and by the time the workers were reached most had died. Waybreaker was commissioned afterward with a simple purpose: no wall of stone should ever be the only reason someone could not be reached.

The weapon contains **3 charges** and regains **1d3 charges at dawn**. While holding Waybreaker, the wielder can place its head against a surface of natural or worked stone, speak the command word, and expend 1 charge. The next strike does not damage the stone normally. Instead, a door-shaped fracture races outward from the point of impact, and the enclosed section of stone folds inward and disappears.

The opening can pass through up to **20 feet of stone** and is large enough for a Medium creature to walk through comfortably. The passage remains open for 10 minutes. When the effect ends, the missing stone returns gradually from the edges inward, giving anyone inside enough warning to leave before the tunnel closes completely.

Waybreaker cannot open a passage through metal, wood, magical barriers, or living creatures. It can pass through worked masonry, fortress walls, cave stone, collapsed tunnels, and similar material. If the hammer cannot reach the far side of the stone within its maximum depth, the charge is not spent.

The command word is a short dwarven phrase meaning **"make room."** It was chosen deliberately. The hammer's makers did not think of the enchantment as destroying stone. They thought of it as convincing stone to temporarily move somewhere else.

The cracks across the hammer's head appeared during its first use. According to the oldest account attached to the weapon, Waybreaker was taken directly from the forge to a mine where another collapse had occurred. Its creator struck the wall before the enchantment had been properly tested. The passage opened successfully, but the hammer's head split under the magical strain.

The cracks never worsened.

Over the following years, dwarven smiths repeatedly attempted to replace the damaged head. Every replacement eventually developed exactly the same fractures in exactly the same places. Eventually they stopped trying. The cracks became accepted as part of the enchantment, evidence that the hammer itself was taking the strain that would otherwise be imposed on the stone around the passage.

Waybreaker eventually became useful outside mines. Surveyors used it to reach sealed caverns. Soldiers used it to bypass blocked tunnels and fortress walls. Rescue crews carried it into collapsed buildings. At least one recorded owner became wealthy by using it to reach mineral deposits that were otherwise too expensive to excavate.

That last use caused considerable arguments about what the hammer was actually for. Some dwarves considered using a rescue tool for prospecting disrespectful. Others pointed out that a tool capable of opening a wall does not particularly care why someone wants the wall opened.

The weapon itself has never shown any preference.

It simply makes room.
