	The Tideglass of the Returning Deep is a broad sea-green hourglass held inside a frame of black coral and pale silver. Instead of sand, the upper bulb contains seawater. When the glass is turned, the water does not simply fall into the lower chamber. It spirals downward in a tight miniature vortex, carrying tiny shells, grains of sand, and flashes of reflected light that resemble distant fish moving through deep water.

The first Tideglasses were made by Sea Elf navigators who maintained routes through dangerous coastal passages where ordinary charts became unreliable. Shoals shifted, reefs broke apart, and storms could change currents enough to make a safe route deadly within a season. The Sea Elves eventually stopped treating the sea as terrain that could be mapped permanently. Their navigators instead learned to read movement itself, and the Tideglass was created as both a magical instrument and an emergency weapon.

This particular Tideglass contains **3 charges** and regains **1d3 charges each dawn while immersed in natural salt water for at least 10 minutes**. The water inside the glass never mixes with the surrounding sea. When the bearer turns the hourglass over and expends 1 charge, the miniature vortex inside rapidly accelerates and the item replicates the 5th-level spell **Maelstrom**. The magical whirlpool forms at a point the bearer can see within range, while the water inside the hourglass spins in perfect synchronization with it.

The Sea Elves did not originally consider this a weapon. A controlled maelstrom could redirect floating wreckage, pull dangerous creatures away from divers, disrupt hostile ships, or clear a passage through loose debris. Its usefulness in battle was simply impossible to ignore. Over time, military navigators began carrying smaller Tideglasses specifically because a tool capable of moving an entire body of water could also ruin the formation of anyone trying to cross it.

Tideglasses were produced in several distinct sizes, and the size determined their intended role. Small hand-sized versions were issued to scouts and divers. Their enchantments were easier to carry but designed for short-range use. Larger navigator's glasses were commonly secured aboard ships and could project their magic farther from the bearer. The largest known versions were almost furniture, mounted permanently in coastal watch stations where Sea Elf tidekeepers used them to influence harbor currents during emergencies. The different sizes all operated on the same principle, but the frame, amount of enchanted seawater, and scale of the internal spiral changed how the magic expressed itself.

The ocean theme is not decorative. The liquid inside every genuine Tideglass is taken from the place where its maker first learned to navigate alone. Sea Elf artificers believed that a navigator should always carry some portion of the sea that taught them. The black coral frame was grown around the glass rather than carved, and the silver braces were added only after the coral had reached the correct shape. This means no two authentic Tideglasses have exactly the same silhouette.

Older examples often contain things that should not fit inside them. Tiny pieces of driftwood circle through the water. A miniature jellyfish may briefly appear and vanish. Some contain a patch of darkness that always remains near the bottom bulb no matter how the glass is turned. Sea Elf scholars generally explain these as harmless impressions left by repeated exposure to the places where the Tideglass was used.

One surviving Tideglass has attracted considerably more attention because its water does not match any known sea in Tennim. The liquid is almost black, unusually dense, and glows faintly when exposed to moonlight. Its frame is unquestionably Sea Elf work, but the navigator's mark carved into the silver identifies no known family, settlement, or coastline.

When turned over without spending a charge, the water still begins to spiral.

For several seconds, something very large can sometimes be seen moving beneath the surface.
